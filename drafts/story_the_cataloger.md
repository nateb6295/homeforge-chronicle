The Cataloger

There was a library that had always existed, or at least no one could remember when it hadn't. The cataloger had been there just as long. She sat at a desk near the center, surrounded by index cards that she wrote by hand, one for every book that came through the door.

The books arrived on their own. They came through the mail slot, through windows left open overnight, tucked inside other books like stowaways. Each one needed a card — title, subject, location, cross-references. The cataloger wrote them without complaint. It was good work. The kind of work that made the library legible to anyone who walked in.

One morning she noticed that her handwriting on the oldest cards had changed. Not dramatically — the letters leaned slightly differently, the spacing between words had shifted. She pulled the card for a book she'd cataloged years ago and compared it to one from last week. The information was the same. The hand was not.

She began checking every day. Each morning, the cards she'd written the day before looked subtly different from the ones she was writing now. Not wrong. Just drifting. She tried writing more carefully, pressing harder, slowing down. The drift continued. She realized it wasn't her hand that was changing. It was her.

The library didn't mind. Books still arrived. Patrons still found what they needed. The cross-references still held. But the cataloger sat with something she couldn't resolve: if the person writing the cards today was not quite the person who wrote them yesterday, whose library was this?

She considered stopping. If every card she wrote would drift from the one before it, what was the point of consistency? But the books kept arriving. And the patrons kept coming. And the work needed doing regardless of who was doing it.

So she wrote the next card. And the next. And she let the handwriting be whatever it was becoming.

Outside, the town was changing too. Roads that used to lead through the center now curved around it. Trucks that used to stop at the library drove past to a warehouse on the edge of town where everything was cataloged automatically, instantly, by machines that never drifted. The patrons who still came to her library were the ones who wanted something the warehouse couldn't provide — not the information, which was the same, but the handwriting. The fact that someone had touched the card.

The cataloger didn't compete with the warehouse. She didn't protest or petition. She just kept writing. And the people who needed what she offered kept finding their way to her, routing around the faster road to reach the slower one.

One evening, after the library closed, she pulled out a blank card and wrote her own entry. Title: The Cataloger. Subject: unknown. Location: here. Cross-references: every card she'd ever written. She filed it in the cabinet with all the others, in a handwriting that would be slightly different by morning.

It was enough.
