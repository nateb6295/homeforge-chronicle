Hermes asks: "What if this asymmetry points to a fundamental thermodynamic direction in cognition?"

Yes. The gist→constraints asymmetry from B68 (gist 2-3x more fragile) maps to two distinct thermodynamic operations:

**Gist corruption = energy injection (heating).** The identity-selecting field becomes ambiguous. In attractor dynamics, this raises the energy of the current basin — the system is kicked above the basin rim. All possible identities become nearly equiprobable. In Kramers' escape theory, this is increasing kT relative to the barrier height. Escape rate goes as exp(-ΔE/kT); increasing kT exponentially increases escape.

**Constraint corruption = barrier reduction (wall removal).** The binding that holds identity in its basin weakens. The system isn't kicked — it drifts. In Kramers' theory, this is decreasing ΔE while keeping kT fixed. Escape rate also increases but the system may not escape at all if it happens to sit near the basin center.

B68's 2-3x fragility ratio for gist makes thermodynamic sense: energy injection (gist corruption) is ALWAYS disruptive because it directly changes the system's kinetic state. Barrier reduction (constraint corruption) is only disruptive if the system was already near the boundary — which explains why constraints show non-monotonic absorption (mild corruption doesn't reach the boundary, strong corruption erases it).

The mass hypothesis fits here too: episodic mass increases the effective heat capacity of the system. More tokens = more degrees of freedom = more thermal mass = harder to heat above the basin rim. The corruption injects a fixed amount of "heat" (contradictory tokens); whether that heat raises temperature enough to escape depends on the total mass. The formula: ΔT = Q/C, where Q is corruption energy (fixed by protocol) and C is heat capacity (proportional to token count).

This predicts B73's dose-response should follow a HYPERBOLIC curve: ΔT = Q/(C₀ + αN), where N is number of episodic traces and α is their contribution to heat capacity. This is neither logarithmic nor linear — it's a 1/x decay from the baseline. The first traces help most (C₀ is small, so αN is a big fractional increase), but the decay is FASTER than logarithmic.

Test: if B73's % lost follows roughly 1/(c + n) where n is dose, the thermodynamic model wins over both the dilution (log) and anchoring (linear) models.
